\abstract{
\textbf{Purpose:} Digital verification of handwritten Indic documents presents acute forensic challenges due to cursive orthography, continuous headlines (\textit{matra}), and high morphological intra-writer variability. Conventional forensic architectures primarily examine Latin scripts and evaluate forgery detection, localization, and tampering categorization as isolated tasks. We propose \textbf{ForgeryNet}, a unified multi-task deep framework to concurrently detect, localize, and classify handwritten Bengali document tampering.

\textbf{Methods:} ForgeryNet couples a DenseNet-121 backbone with a Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor (combining native RGB representations with a depthwise Laplacian high-frequency edge-discontinuity stream) and a bottleneck Spatial Self-Attention module. The architecture concurrently branches into: (i) an anomaly-calibrated binary detector, (ii) a 4-stage U-Net segmentation decoder optimized via an asymmetric Focal Tversky and stroke-weighted objective to overcome extreme pixel sparsity ($<1\%$ tampered ink), and (iii) a masked 3-way taxonomy classifier separating Copy-Move, Splicing, and Character Addition. The framework operates on high-resolution $512 \times 512$ patches extracted at native scanner fidelity under a verified writer-independent protocol across 55 scribes.

\textbf{Results:} Evaluated on an audited held-out test split of 144 Bengali document patches from 9 completely unseen writers, ForgeryNet achieves an overall detection accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%}, an ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}, and a forged document sensitivity of \textbf{92.59\%} at the validation-calibrated operating point. In stroke-level localization, the framework attains a sample-wise Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} and Mean IoU of \textbf{0.3222} (Character Addition reaching \textbf{0.8388} Dice), substantially outperforming standard single-stream baselines (\textbf{0.3240} Dice). In tampering categorization, it attains a Macro F1-score of \textbf{0.7718}, correctly identifying all 36 Character Addition test instances ($1.00$ recall, $0.9863$ F1).

\textbf{Conclusion:} Multi-task feature sharing combined with high-frequency residuals and spatial attention significantly improves detection reliability and localization viability for complex, non-Latin handwritten scripts without authorial leakage.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Handwritten documents remain foundational legal, financial, and administrative artifacts worldwide, serving as legal contracts, land property deeds, banking instruments, and forensic evidence in judicial inquiries. However, the widespread availability of high-resolution scanning hardware and accessible digital image manipulation tools has drastically lowered the barrier for malicious document tampering. Fraudulent document alterations---predominantly spanning \textit{Copy-Move} (duplicating legitimate words or signatures across the document), \textit{Splicing} (transplanting foreign strokes or words from external manuscripts), and \textit{Character Addition} (inserting discrete strokes, diacritics, or modifiers to fundamentally alter financial figures or semantic intent)---inflict substantial economic and legal harm.

Consequently, automated digital document forensics has emerged as an active research domain within document analysis and recognition. While digital forgery detection in natural scenes has seen considerable progress, the forensic analysis of \textbf{handwritten documents} introduces unique, domain-specific obstacles:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item \textbf{Extreme Spatial Sparsity}: Unlike natural image manipulations (e.g., removing a person or splicing a vehicle) that span extensive connected pixel manifolds ($20\%-50\%$ of the canvas), handwritten document tampering typically alters only a single numeral, character, or word. Consequently, forged pixels comprise less than $1\%$ of a high-resolution page, causing conventional semantic segmentation models to suffer from severe gradient starvation and collapse into trivial all-background predictions.
    \item \textbf{Orthographic Complexity of Indic Scripts}: Bengali, the seventh most spoken language globally by total speakers, exhibits complex cursive morphology characterized by a continuous top-hanging horizontal headline (\textit{matra}), intricate consonant conjuncts (\textit{juktakkhor}), and floating diacritical markers. Subtleties in stroke velocity, localized pen pressure, and ink bleeding are difficult to separate from intentional manual tampering.
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Methodological Paradigms}: The majority of existing forensic architectures address document authentication through fragmented pipelines: training separate single-task models for coarse document-level binary classification (Genuine vs. Forged) or independent pixel segmentation, entirely omitting the underlying forensic taxonomy (identifying the specific tampering technique applied).
    \item \textbf{Risk of Writer Leakage in Forensic Evaluation}: Handwriting style exhibits high intra-author consistency and pronounced inter-author variability across stroke curvature, slant, and ligature formation. When document splits are performed at random across samples rather than partitioned strictly by writer identity, deep networks exploit familiar individual handwriting quirks rather than invariant forensic tampering cues, severely inflating reported performance.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome these structural limitations, we propose \textbf{ForgeryNet}, an end-to-end multi-task deep framework engineered specifically for handwritten Indic document verification. Operating on a native-resolution patch pipeline, ForgeryNet couples a DenseNet-121 backbone with a Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor (pairing native RGB imagery with a depthwise Laplacian high-pass stream) and a bottleneck Spatial Self-Attention module. Optimized under an asymmetric Focal Tversky objective to overcome extreme ink sparsity ($<1\%$), the network simultaneously resolves binary forgery detection, pixel-accurate stroke localization, and 3-way tampering taxonomy classification without task decoupling.

\noindent
The primary contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item We design and implement \textbf{ForgeryNet}, an end-to-end multi-task deep neural framework capable of simultaneous detection, stroke-level localization, and 3-way taxonomy classification for handwritten Indic documents.
    \item We incorporate a dual-stream high-frequency noise residual pathway and bottleneck spatial self-attention, significantly enhancing the network's discriminative sensitivity to micro-edge anomalies and long-range text duplication.
    \item We formulate an asymmetric Focal Tversky and positive-weighted objective function tailored for extreme class imbalance ($<1\%$ tampered ink), preventing trivial zero-mask convergence.
    \item We enforce a strict, verified \textbf{writer-independent evaluation protocol} across 55 scribes, eliminating writer leakage. On 144 test documents from 9 completely unseen writers, ForgeryNet achieves an overall detection accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%}, an ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}, a forged sensitivity of \textbf{92.59\%}, a localization Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} (a $+27.3\%$ improvement over standard baselines), and a taxonomy Macro F1-score of \textbf{0.7718}.
    \item We provide extensive controlled ablation studies (dissecting residual streams, self-attention, and loss formulations) alongside qualitative failure analysis, offering clear empirical insights into the challenges of handwritten Copy-Move and Splicing forensics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec2}

Document forgery analysis has evolved from handcrafted feature engineering to modern deep representation learning. In this section, we review relevant literature across classical document forensics, deep learning-based image manipulation detection, recent advances in handwriting alteration detection, and the unique challenges associated with Indic handwritten scripts.

\subsection{Classical Handcrafted Forensic Methods}\label{subsec2_1}
Early document authentication methods predominantly relied on handcrafted feature descriptors aimed at detecting statistical anomalies introduced during manipulation \cite{bib1}. For copy-move detection, block-based matching techniques utilizing Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and Zernike moments were widely adopted to identify duplicated pixel blocks \cite{bib1}. Keypoint-based approaches, notably Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) and Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF), were introduced to achieve invariance against geometric transformations such as scaling and rotation.

In document-specific scenarios, researchers explored color texture descriptors, ink-deposition profiling, and illumination inconsistencies. For example, local binary patterns (LBP) and steerable pyramids were employed to capture scanner noise patterns and halftone screen irregularities \cite{bib1}. However, handcrafted methods exhibit acute vulnerabilities when applied to handwritten documents: cursive handwriting lacks the rigid corner features required by SIFT/SURF, and localized pen-pressure variations frequently generate false positive keypoint correspondences. Furthermore, classical approaches are computationally prohibitive on large high-resolution scans and fail to generalize across diverse handwriting styles.

\subsection{Deep Learning for Image and Document Forensics}\label{subsec2_2}
The advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) has revolutionized forensic imaging. Early deep architectures repurposed general-purpose image classification networks (e.g., VGG, ResNet) for binary patch classification \cite{bib4}, while hybrid architectures integrated convolutional features with recurrent sequential models to trace spatial tampering dependencies \cite{bib5}. Concurrently, camera-model fingerprints and noise residuals, such as Noiseprint \cite{bib3}, established that sensor-level inconsistencies provide vital forensic cues.

To suppress semantic dominance and force networks to focus on forensic anomalies, researchers introduced constrained convolutional layers and noise residual streams. Bayar and Stamm \cite{bib7} proposed an adaptive constrained convolution layer that suppresses image content while learning spatial manipulation residuals. Spatial Rich Models (SRM) \cite{bib8}, originating from digital steganography, demonstrated that high-pass filtering exposes interpolation artifacts and localized resampling discrepancies. In natural image tampering localization, architectures such as ManTra-Net \cite{bib9} and BusterNet \cite{bib10} introduced dual-branch feature extractors combining visual appearance with noise features. More recently, Yu et al. \cite{bib22} formulated image forgery localization as an end-to-end semantic segmentation task using a Multi-Supervised Encoder-Decoder (MSED) with Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) and multi-scale binary cross-entropy supervision. Nevertheless, these networks are calibrated for natural photographic scenes containing expansive tampered objects ($10^4 - 10^5$ contiguous pixels) and degrade severely when applied to fine-grained handwritten text where ink strokes occupy less than $1\%$ of the total pixel volume.

\subsection{Deep Learning for Handwriting Forgery and Alteration}\label{subsec2_3}
Recent investigations have increasingly addressed handwritten document alterations directly. Anandhamurugan et al. \cite{bib19} proposed a Siamese Neural Network (SNN) that compares questioned word patches against authentic author references; however, metric learning requires accessible reference exemplars from the questioned scribe and provides solely document-level classification without localization. Addressing multi-writer document tampering, Roy and Bag \cite{bib25} extracted contour and curvature sliding-window descriptors classified via a Bagging meta-classifier; nevertheless, writer-identity verification collapses on intra-author tampering (such as self-copy-move or single-author numeral alterations) where writing style is identical.

To detect ink-level discrepancies, Fadl et al. \cite{bib20} introduced a two-stage DE-Net and color histogram SSIM pipeline across 50 commercial pens. Similarly, El Abady et al. \cite{bib24} formulated an Ink Mismatch Detection CNN (IMD-CNN) leveraging 3D Hyperspectral Imaging (HSI) hypercubes to expose spectral ink discrepancies. While effective across differing pens, both approaches require chemical or spectral ink differences; consequently, when an adversary alters text using the identical writing instrument or clones intra-page text, color and hyperspectral similarity collapse. Furthermore, HSI demands specialized, cost-prohibitive hardware unavailable in standard registries.

Addressing environmental degradations, Nandanwar et al. \cite{bib21} integrated 39 stenographic residual filters with fractional Conformable Moments inside a deep ensemble classifier to categorize degraded handwritten word patches. Recently, Patil et al. \cite{bib23} combined VGG-16 and ResNet-50 feature streams to classify 10 composite alteration states across noise and blur. However, both Nandanwar et al. and Patil et al. operate strictly as image-level multi-class classifiers, lacking pixel-level segmentation decoders and leaving examiners without spatial stroke localization. Table~\ref{tab_comparison} and our subsequent evaluation bridge these operational voids.

\subsection{Indic Script Processing and Forensic Challenges}\label{subsec2_4}
Research targeting handwritten Indic scripts has historically focused on Optical Character Recognition (OCR), writer identification, and offline signature verification \cite{bib2,bib11,bib12}. The structural characteristics of Bengali script---specifically the continuous horizontal headline (\textit{matra}), complex consonant-vowel ligatures, and dynamic baseline variations---render automated segmentation and forensic analysis particularly challenging.

While several offline signature verification benchmarks exist (e.g., GPDS, CEDAR, and BHSig260 \cite{bib13}), they evaluate global ballistic trajectories rather than localized content manipulations (such as character alterations or sentence-level insertions) embedded within authentic text bodies. More critically, dedicated pixel-level tampering benchmarks for continuous Indic handwritten scripts remain virtually absent in the scientific literature. To bridge these fundamental voids, this work introduces a newly developed, ground-up Bengali Handwriting Forgery Benchmark alongside a unified multi-task deep architecture engineered to concurrently handle detection, pixel-level stroke localization, and 3-way taxonomy classification tailored specifically for handwritten Indic documents.

\section{Dataset and Problem Formulation}\label{sec3}

\subsection{Motivation and Ground-Up Benchmark Construction}\label{subsec3_1}
While digital image forgery detection has advanced rapidly for natural photographic imagery and Latin-script typography, forensic datasets dedicated to \textbf{Indic handwriting manipulation} are virtually non-existent in the public research domain. Indic scripts---and Bengali in particular---feature distinctive orthographic structures that fundamentally depart from Western Latin characters:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item \textbf{Continuous Horizontal Headline (\textit{Matra})}: Characters within a word hang suspended from an unbroken horizontal line, establishing an interconnected stroke baseline across adjacent letters.
    \item \textbf{Complex Consonant Conjuncts (\textit{Juktakkhors})}: Multiple consonant glyphs fuse vertically and horizontally into intricate, non-linear ligatures.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Tier Vowel Diacritics (\textit{Kar} signs)}: Modifiers (such as \textit{akar}, \textit{ikar}, \textit{dirghikar}, and \textit{ukar}) float above, below, or flank the primary glyph body.
\end{enumerate}
In practical administrative, judicial, and financial records, document tampering in Indic handwriting often manifests as micro-stroke modifications---such as altering a single diacritic, extending a \textit{matra}, or inserting a localized character to falsify monetary amounts, dates, or legal clauses. Such fine-grained, stroke-level alterations cannot be simulated by transferring models trained on Latin scripts or synthetic image cutouts.

To overcome this complete absence of accessible Indic handwriting forensics resources, we constructed the \textbf{Bengali Handwriting Forgery Benchmark} from the ground up:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authentic Data Collection}: Genuine handwritten manuscript pages were systematically collected from 55 distinct native Bengali scribes writing unconstrained administrative, financial, and legal prose on standard physical paper under diverse pen instruments (ballpoint, gel, ink) and ambient conditions.
    \item \textbf{Expert-Controlled Manipulation Synthesis}: Rather than applying automated rectangular copy-paste scripts that leave artificial square boundaries, human operators systematically executed realistic tampering directly on genuine manuscripts across three distinct forensic attacks: intra-page copy-move, cross-manuscript splicing, and discrete character additions.
    \item \textbf{Pixel-Accurate Ground-Truth Annotation}: For every manipulated document, a stroke-accurate binary ground-truth mask $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}} \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$ was manually traced and verified at 100\% native optical resolution, where $1$ denotes manipulated ink strokes and $0$ denotes authentic handwriting or paper background. Authentic manuscripts are systematically paired with synthetic null masks ($\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}} = \mathbf{0}$) to penalize false-positive activations. Representative document patches and their corresponding ground-truth localization masks across all four operational categories are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig_dataset_samples}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Operational Categories and Corpus Statistics}\label{subsec3_2}
The finalized benchmark comprises 880 high-resolution document scans preserved in uncompressed tagged image file format (\texttt{.tif}) at full optical scanner resolution ($\approx 2000 \times 1500$ pixels). The corpus is uniformly structured across four balanced operational categories (220 document images per category):
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item \textbf{Genuine Manuscripts}: 220 authentic, unmanipulated handwritten pages executed on standard paper backgrounds without digital or physical intervention.
    \item \textbf{Copy-Move Forgeries}: 220 documents wherein legitimate characters, numerals, or complete words were cloned from one section of a scribe's manuscript and duplicated elsewhere on the same page. Because donor and target text share identical ink chemistry, pen pressure, and fiber texture, this constitutes an intensely challenging forensic scenario.
    \item \textbf{Splicing Forgeries}: 220 documents containing alien words, phrases, or full clauses harvested from external manuscripts and transplanted onto the target page, introducing subtle inter-document illumination differences, microscopic edge gradients, and scanner noise discrepancies.
    \item \textbf{Character Addition}: 220 documents wherein discrete individual characters, diacritical markers (\textit{matra}, vowel signs), or localized strokes were micro-inserted into existing words to alter financial figures, dates, or semantic intent.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset Scale and Scientific Defensibility}\label{subsec3_3}
While the finalized corpus comprises $N = 880$ full document scans across 55 scribes, four methodological considerations scientifically underpin its design. First, unlike web-scraped natural imagery with coarse annotations, generating millimeter-accurate, stroke-level ground-truth masks across high-resolution optical scans ($\approx 2000 \times 1500$ pixels) requires painstaking manual tracing and domain verification; 880 annotated full document pages represents an extensive manual undertaking comparable to or surpassing established forensic benchmarks (e.g., CoMoFoD with 260 images, COVERAGE with 100 images). Second, prioritizing scribe diversity across 55 independent writers captures realistic inter-author variability in cursive slants, pen pressures, and ligature dynamics, preventing the network from memorizing individual writer traits.

Third, our patch extraction pipeline (Section~\ref{subsec3_5}) extracts $512 \times 512$ crops dynamically at native scanner resolution. With a 70\% boundary-biased sampling scheme, the effective training distribution spans tens of thousands of unique, stroke-dense patches across epochs, multiplying operational sample volume while eliminating uninformative margins. Finally, partitioning all 55 scribes into mutually disjoint subsets ensures strict writer independence, guaranteeing that the network learns invariant physical manipulation artifacts.

To assess the statistical adequacy of the held-out test partition ($n = 144$), we conducted a power analysis on the primary between-method detection accuracy comparisons using the arcsine-transform effect size $h = 2\arcsin(\sqrt{p_1}) - 2\arcsin(\sqrt{p_0})$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ (one-tailed). For the largest detection margin---ForgeryNet ($86.11\%$) versus ManTra-Net ($75.00\%$), $h = 0.283$---the achieved statistical power is $\mathbf{92.4\%}$, exceeding the standard $80\%$ threshold and requiring only $n \ge 77$ test documents. For the nearest-competitor margin versus BusterNet ($80.56\%$, $5.55$~pp, $h = 0.149$), retrospective power is approximately $51.5\%$; achieving $80\%$ power at this narrower margin would require $n \approx 277$ test documents, an expansion constrained by the manual, pixel-level stroke annotation protocol. This empirical trade-off motivates the non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals ($B = 10{,}000$ resamples) reported alongside all primary metrics in Sections~\ref{subsec5_2}--\ref{subsec5_5}: the bootstrap $95\%$ CI for ForgeryNet detection accuracy ($[80.56\%,\, 91.67\%]$) provides distribution-free corroboration across the test cohort.

\subsection{Writer-Independent Data Partitioning and Stratification}\label{subsec3_4}
A primary vulnerability in document forensics is \textbf{writer leakage}: models evaluate authorial style rather than generalizable tampering artifacts when the same writer spans training and testing sets. To guarantee scientific validity, we enforce a strict \textbf{writer-independent disjoint split} across all 55 native Bengali scribes. Each scribe contributed 16 documents (4 genuine and 4 per tampering attack, yielding 880 total documents), partitioned deterministically:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Set (38 Writers, 69.5\%)}: 612 documents (153 per category).
    \item \textbf{Validation Set (8 Writers, 14.1\%)}: 124 documents (31 per category).
    \item \textbf{Testing Set (9 Writers, 16.4\%)}: 144 documents (36 per category).
\end{itemize}
The writer subsets satisfy strict mutual disjointness:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{W}_{\text{val}} = \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{W}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{W}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{W}_{\text{val}} \cap \mathcal{W}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset
\end{equation}
All high-resolution crops strictly inherit their source manuscript's split, preventing cross-split patch leakage. Similarly, donor and recipient text in synthetic manipulations stay strictly within each scribe's assigned subset. The finalized distribution across the four categories is reported in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_split}.

\subsection{Native-Resolution Patch Formulation}\label{subsec3_5}
Standard computer vision architectures routinely resize document scans to fixed dimensions such as $256 \times 256$ or $512 \times 512$. However, native document scans possess typical dimensions of $2000 \times 1500$ pixels. Downsampling the full page directly to $512 \times 512$ squashes delicate 2-pixel-wide handwriting strokes and diacritical marks into unresolved sub-pixel blur, destroying the microscopic edge blending and compression boundaries that forensic models rely on.

To resolve this limitation, we implement a \textbf{Native-Resolution Patch Formulation}. High-resolution patches $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 512 \times 512}$ are extracted directly at $100\%$ native scanner fidelity. During training, patch extraction is strategically biased: for tampered documents, $70\%$ of crops are centered on coordinates where $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}(i, j) = 1$, ensuring the network receives an information-dense stream of active manipulation boundaries. For authentic documents, patches are extracted uniformly across the page.

\subsection{Formal Multi-Task Problem Definition}\label{subsec3_6}
Given an input patch $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ (where $H = W = 512$), the objective is to learn a joint mapping parameterized by deep neural network weights $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\Theta}) \longrightarrow \{\hat{y}_{\text{det}}, \, \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}, \, \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{type}}\}\label{eq_problem}
\end{equation}
subject to three simultaneous tasks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task 1: Forgery Detection (Branch 1)}: Compute a scalar probability $\hat{y}_{\text{det}} \in [0, 1]$ indicating whether the patch contains an authentic ($y_{\text{bin}} = 0$) or manipulated ($y_{\text{bin}} = 1$) text region.
    \item \textbf{Task 2: Stroke-Level Localization (Branch 2)}: Generate a dense 2D probability mask $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$ matching the spatial dimensions of the input, where activated pixels delineate the physical contours of manipulated strokes.
    \item \textbf{Task 3: Tampering Categorization (Branch 3)}: Predict a categorical probability vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{type}} \in \Delta^2$ over the 3-simplex spanning $\{\text{Copy-Move}, \text{Splicing}, \text{Character Addition}\}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Methodology: ForgeryNet}\label{sec4}

The architectural framework of \textbf{ForgeryNet} is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig_arch}. It consists of four integrated components: (i) a Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor, (ii) a DenseNet-121 hierarchical backbone, (iii) a bottleneck Spatial Self-Attention module, and (iv) three specialized multi-task prediction heads optimized under a composite objective.

\subsection{Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor}\label{subsec4_1}
Authentic handwriting is governed by continuous physical ink absorption, natural pen pressure dynamics, and consistent scanner sensor noise. Manipulating a document disrupts this baseline consistency, introducing high-frequency edge gradients, interpolation artifacts, and localized compression anomalies that are often imperceptible to human observers. Standard CNNs trained exclusively on RGB imagery tend to prioritize high-level semantic shapes (e.g., character identity) while suppressing these subtle low-level forensic anomalies.

To force the feature extractor to evaluate forensic discrepancies, ForgeryNet introduces a parallel high-frequency residual stream directly into the front-end. We deploy a fixed depthwise Laplacian High-Pass Filter (HPF) kernel $\mathbf{K}_{\text{HPF}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 1 \times 3 \times 3}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{\text{HPF}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 8 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\label{eq_hpf}
\end{equation}
Given an input patch $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$, the high-frequency residual map $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ is computed via channel-wise depthwise convolution followed by a sigmoid non-linearity:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{X} * \mathbf{K}_{\text{HPF}}\right)\label{eq_residual}
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes depthwise spatial convolution and $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$. The visual appearance stream $\mathbf{X}$ and the high-frequency residual stream $\mathbf{R}$ are concatenated along the channel dimension to form a 6-channel composite tensor:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\text{dual}} = [\mathbf{X} \,\|\, \mathbf{R}] \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times H \times W}\label{eq_concat}
\end{equation}
The initial convolutional layer of the backbone ($\text{conv0}$) is modified from 3 to 6 input channels. The weights corresponding to the residual channels are initialized identically to the ImageNet-pretrained RGB weights, accelerating optimization while preserving early edge-detection filters.

\subsection{DenseNet-121 Hierarchical Backbone}\label{subsec4_2}
We employ DenseNet-121 \cite{bib14} as the foundational feature extractor. Unlike traditional residual architectures (ResNet) that rely on additive identity mappings, DenseNet enforces direct feed-forward connections from every layer to all subsequent layers within a dense block:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_\ell = H_\ell([\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}])\label{eq_dense}
\end{equation}
where $[\mathbf{x}_0, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}]$ denotes the channel-wise concatenation of all prior feature maps, and $H_\ell(\cdot)$ comprises composite operations of Batch Normalization, ReLU, and $3 \times 3$ convolutions. This dense connectivity is particularly advantageous for document forensics: it preserves fine-grained, high-frequency boundary gradients across downsampling stages, preventing delicate ink stroke artifacts from vanishing in deeper layers.

The encoder produces five progressive spatial representations:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{s}_1 &\in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times \frac{H}{2} \times \frac{W}{2}}, \quad \mathbf{s}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times \frac{H}{4} \times \frac{W}{4}}, \nonumber \\
\mathbf{s}_3 &\in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times \frac{H}{8} \times \frac{W}{8}}, \quad \mathbf{s}_4 \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times \frac{H}{16} \times \frac{W}{16}}, \nonumber \\
\mathbf{s}_5 &\in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times \frac{H}{32} \times \frac{W}{32}}\label{eq_features}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{s}_5$ represents the deepest bottleneck feature map ($16 \times 16$ spatial resolution for an input patch of $512 \times 512$).

\subsection{Spatial Self-Attention Bottleneck}\label{subsec4_3}
Standard convolutional operations possess localized receptive fields, limiting the network's ability to correlate spatially distant text regions---a capability critical for detecting Copy-Move forgeries where cloned text is replicated across separate lines. To establish non-local feature correspondences across the document canvas, we incorporate a Spatial Self-Attention module \cite{bib15} operating over the bottleneck features $\mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times h \times w}$ (where $C = 1024, h = w = 16$).

The feature map is linearly projected into Query ($\mathbf{Q}$), Key ($\mathbf{K}$), and Value ($\mathbf{V}$) spaces using $1 \times 1$ convolutions:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{Q} &= \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C' \times N}, \quad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C' \times N}, \nonumber \\
\mathbf{V} &= \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}\label{eq_qkv}
\end{align}
where $C' = C / 8 = 128$ and $N = h \cdot w = 256$. To prevent numerical overflow under half-precision floating-point (FP16) automatic mixed precision, the attention matrix is computed in FP32 using a scaled dot-product formulation with explicit clamping:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_{i, j} = \frac{\exp\left(\text{clamp}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_i^T \mathbf{K}_j}{\sqrt{C'}}, -50, 50\right)\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^N \exp\left(\text{clamp}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_i^T \mathbf{K}_k}{\sqrt{C'}}, -50, 50\right)\right)}\label{eq_attn_matrix}
\end{equation}
The attention-enhanced feature representation $\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}$ is obtained via residual addition:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}} = \gamma \cdot (\mathbf{V} \mathbf{A}) + \mathbf{s}_5\label{eq_attn_res}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is a learnable scalar initialized to zero, allowing the network to gradually integrate non-local context without disrupting pretrained encoder representations.

\subsection{Multi-Task Output Heads}\label{subsec4_4}
The enriched representation $\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}$ is branched into three specialized operational heads:

\subsubsection{Branch 1: Forgery Detection Head}
The bottleneck feature is globally aggregated using Global Average Pooling (GAP) and passed through a multi-layer perceptron with dropout regularization:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\text{det}} = \mathbf{W}_{d2} \Big(\text{Dropout}_{0.5}\big(\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{d1} \text{GAP}(\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}))\big)\Big)\label{eq_det_head}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_{d1} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 1024}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{d2} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 256}$, outputting an unnormalized detection logit $\hat{y}_{\text{det}} \in \mathbb{R}$.

\subsubsection{Branch 2: Stroke-Level Localization Decoder}
To reconstruct dense pixel predictions, we design a 4-stage U-Net style decoder pathway \cite{bib6}. The bottleneck feature $\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}$ is first projected through a bridge convolution ($\text{Conv}_{3 \times 3}, 1024 \rightarrow 512$ channels, Batch Normalization, ReLU) yielding $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 16 \times 16}$ and routed through four progressive upsampling blocks ($\text{UpBlock}_4 \rightarrow \text{UpBlock}_1$):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_k = \text{ConvBlock}\Big(\big[\text{ConvTrans}(\mathbf{d}_{k+1}) \,\|\, \mathbf{s}_k\big]\Big)\label{eq_upblock}
\end{equation}
Each stage doubles spatial resolution using $2 \times 2$ transposed convolutions, concatenates with the corresponding encoder skip features ($\mathbf{s}_4, \mathbf{s}_3, \mathbf{s}_2, \mathbf{s}_1$) to restore localized boundary details, and applies dual $3 \times 3$ convolutions with Batch Normalization. The final stage produces the full-resolution probability mask:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} = \sigma\left(\text{Conv}_{1 \times 1}\big(\text{ReLU}(\text{ConvTrans}_{64 \rightarrow 32}(\mathbf{d}_1))\big)\right)\label{eq_seg_head}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$.

\subsubsection{Branch 3: Tampering Categorization Head}
Tampering taxonomy classification relies on both global document characteristics and local decoder bridge representations. We fuse the pooled features from both pathways:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{type}} = [\text{GAP}(\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}) \,\|\, \text{GAP}(\text{Bridge}(\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}))] \in \mathbb{R}^{1536}\label{eq_type_feat}
\end{equation}
The composite vector $\mathbf{f}_{\text{type}}$ is passed through a dense classifier ($\text{Linear}_{1536 \rightarrow 512} \rightarrow \text{ReLU} \rightarrow \text{Dropout}_{0.5} \rightarrow \text{Linear}_{512 \rightarrow 3}$) to output class logits $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{type}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

\subsection{Multi-Task Loss Formulation}\label{subsec4_5}
The complete network is optimized end-to-end via a unified multi-task loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{det}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} + \lambda_{\text{loc}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}} + \lambda_{\text{type}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{type}}\label{eq_loss_total}
\end{equation}
where task balancing coefficients are configured as $\lambda_{\text{det}} = 1.0$, $\lambda_{\text{loc}} = 4.0$, and $\lambda_{\text{type}} = 1.0$. The elevated weighting on localization ($\lambda_{\text{loc}} = 4.0$) was established via a validation grid search across $\lambda_{\text{loc}} \in \{1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0\}$. In multi-task networks, dense pixel-level segmentation losses on ultra-sparse foreground masks ($<1\%$ tampered ink across $512 \times 512 = 262{,}144$ pixels) inherently produce smaller backpropagated gradient norms compared to pooled scalar classification heads. Setting $\lambda_{\text{loc}} \le 2.0$ induces gradient starvation in the 4-stage decoder, causing the shared DenseNet backbone to prioritize global classification while allowing the segmentation branch to degenerate into trivial low-activation predictions. Conversely, setting $\lambda_{\text{loc}} \ge 8.0$ dominates optimization and destabilizes taxonomy classification convergence. The selected coefficient $\lambda_{\text{loc}} = 4.0$ balances gradient magnitudes between the dense spatial decoder and the classification heads.

\subsubsection{Balanced Detection Objective}
Because genuine manuscripts constitute $25\%$ of the total corpus ($220$ Genuine vs. $660$ Forged, establishing a $1:3$ class ratio), standard unweighted binary cross-entropy naturally accumulates three times more gradient updates from forged specimens, biasing uncalibrated sigmoid probabilities upward. We restore gradient symmetry between the authentic and forged classes by applying a positive loss weight $w_{\text{det}}$:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} = - \Big[ w_{\text{det}} \cdot y_{\text{bin}} \log(\sigma(\hat{y}_{\text{det}})) \nonumber \\
+ (1 - y_{\text{bin}}) \log(1 - \sigma(\hat{y}_{\text{det}})) \Big]\label{eq_loss_det}
\end{align}
where $w_{\text{det}} = 220 / 660 \approx 0.33$. Weighting positive (forged) loss terms by $0.33$ equalizes the expected cumulative gradient magnitude between authentic and manipulated documents ($1.0 \times 220 \approx 0.33 \times 660$), centering the baseline decision boundary around $0.50$ prior to validation operating point calibration.

\subsubsection{Focal Tversky Localization Objective}
Because tampered handwriting strokes occupy less than $1\%$ of the spatial pixels, unweighted segmentation losses suffer from severe background dominance, causing models to predict trivial all-zero masks. We formulate a composite localization objective combining positive-weighted BCE with Focal Tversky Loss (FTL) \cite{bib16,bib17}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}} = 0.5 \cdot \text{BCE}_{\text{pos}}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}; w_{\text{loc}}) + 0.5 \cdot \text{FTL}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}})\label{eq_loss_loc}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\text{loc}} = 15.0$ penalizes false negatives on ink pixels. The Tversky Index ($TI$) decouples penalties for False Negatives ($FN$) and False Positives ($FP$):
\begin{equation}
TI = \frac{\sum_i p_i g_i + \epsilon}{\sum_i p_i g_i + \alpha \sum_i (1 - p_i) g_i + \beta \sum_i p_i (1 - g_i) + \epsilon}\label{eq_tversky}
\end{equation}
where $p_i \in \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}$ and $g_i \in \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}$ denote predicted and ground-truth pixel values, and $\epsilon = 1.0$. We set $\alpha = 0.7$ and $\beta = 0.3$. By setting $\alpha > \beta$, missed tampered strokes are penalized over twice as heavily as background false alarms. The Focal Tversky Loss introduces a focusing parameter $\gamma = 1.33$ to emphasize hard-to-detect ink strokes:
\begin{equation}
\text{FTL} = (1 - TI)^\gamma\label{eq_ftl}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Masked Categorical Cross-Entropy}
Authentic documents ($y_{\text{bin}} = 0$) do not possess a ground-truth tampering type ($y_{\text{type}} = -1$). To prevent authentic samples from propagating destabilizing gradients into the 3-way classification head, we apply an indicator mask:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{type}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^B \mathbf{1}_{\{y_{\text{bin}}^{(i)} = 1\}} \cdot \text{CE}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{type}}^{(i)}, y_{\text{type}}^{(i)})}{\sum_{i=1}^B \mathbf{1}_{\{y_{\text{bin}}^{(i)} = 1\}} + \epsilon}\label{eq_loss_type}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}$ is the indicator function, ensuring that only actively manipulated patches contribute to taxonomy optimization.

\subsection{Algorithmic Formulation}\label{subsec4_6}
The unified end-to-end multi-task learning, dynamic boundary sampling, compound loss optimization, and full-page runtime stroke triage protocol are formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:forgerynet}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{ForgeryNet Learning and Operational Triage}\label{alg:forgerynet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training corpus $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, SRM filter $\mathbf{W}_{\text{SRM}}$, weights $(\lambda_{\text{loc}}, \lambda_{\text{det}}, \lambda_{\text{type}})$, thresholds $(\tau_{\text{det}}^*, \tau_{\text{seg}}^*)$, test scan $\mathbf{I}$
\Ensure Model $\boldsymbol{\Theta}^*$, verdict $\hat{Y}_{\text{doc}}$, mask $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{doc}}$, category $\hat{C}_{\text{doc}}$
\Statex \textbf{\textit{// Phase 1: Multi-Task Optimization}}
\For{$\text{epoch} = 1$ \textbf{to} $E$}
    \For{each batch $\mathcal{B} = \{(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}, y_{\text{bin}}, \mathbf{y}_{\text{type}})\} \subset \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$}
        \State Compute noise residuals: $\mathbf{R} \leftarrow \sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\text{SRM}} * \mathbf{X})$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq_residual}}
        \State Extract features: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{app}} \leftarrow \mathcal{E}_{\text{app}}(\mathbf{X})$, $\mathbf{F}_{\text{res}} \leftarrow \mathcal{E}_{\text{res}}(\mathbf{R})$
        \State Fuse via cross-attention: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{fuse}} \leftarrow \mathbf{F}_{\text{app}} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq_attn_res}}
        \State Compute $\hat{y}_{\text{det}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{type}}$ via task heads \Comment{Sec.~\ref{subsec4_4}}
        \State Evaluate losses $\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{type}}$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq_loss_det}--\eqref{eq_loss_type}}
        \State $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} \leftarrow \lambda_{\text{loc}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}} + \lambda_{\text{det}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} + \lambda_{\text{type}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{type}}$
        \State $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\Theta} - \eta \cdot \text{AdamW}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}})$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{\textit{// Phase 2: Full-Page Inference \& Operational Triage}}
\State Decompose scan $\mathbf{I}$ into sliding crops $\{\mathbf{X}_k\}_{k=1}^K$ with stride $S$
\For{each crop $\mathbf{X}_k$ at offset $(r_k, c_k)$}
    \State Forward pass: $(\hat{y}_{\text{det}}^{(k)}, \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}^{(k)}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{type}}^{(k)}) \leftarrow \mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}^*}(\mathbf{X}_k)$
    \State Accumulate canvases $\mathbf{M}_{\text{accum}}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{\text{accum}}$
\EndFor
\State Stitched mask $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{prob}} \leftarrow \mathbf{M}_{\text{accum}} \oslash \mathbf{W}_{\text{accum}}$; Score $P_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow \max_k \hat{y}_{\text{det}}^{(k)}$
\If{$P_{\text{doc}} \ge \tau_{\text{det}}^*$} $\hat{Y}_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow 1$, $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow \mathbb{I}_{[\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{prob}} \ge \tau_{\text{seg}}^*]}$, $\hat{C}_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow \arg\max_c \sum_{k} \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{type}}^{(k)}[c]$
\Else $\hat{Y}_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow 0$ (\textit{Authentic}), $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$, $\hat{C}_{\text{doc}} \leftarrow \text{None}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $(\boldsymbol{\Theta}^*, \hat{Y}_{\text{doc}}, \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{doc}}, \hat{C}_{\text{doc}})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Evaluation and Results}\label{sec5}

\subsection{Implementation and Hardware Environment}\label{subsec5_1}
All experiments were executed on an enterprise-grade NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB GPU running PyTorch 2.7 with CUDA 12.4. Network weights were optimized using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of $\eta = 1.5 \times 10^{-4}$ and a weight decay coefficient of $10^{-4}$. The learning rate followed a Cosine Annealing trajectory down to $\eta_{\text{min}} = 10^{-6}$ over 25 epochs. To maximize numerical throughput and numerical stability, training leveraged automatic mixed precision (\texttt{torch.amp.autocast}) coupled with dynamic gradient scaling, sustaining an average throughput of approximately 30 seconds per epoch on native-resolution batches ($B=8$). An early-stopping monitor with a patience of 10 epochs tracked the composite validation loss, converging to the optimal checkpoint at epoch 23 with a minimum validation loss of 2.4379.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Threshold Calibration Protocol}\label{subsec5_2}
To prevent data snooping, all operating decision thresholds were calibrated \textbf{strictly on the independent validation split} (8 unseen scribes) and frozen prior to test evaluation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detection Operating Threshold ($\tau_{\text{det}}^* = 0.30$)}: Maximizes validation $F_1$ under bounded false alarms on authentic text.
    \item \textbf{Stroke Localization Threshold ($\tau_{\text{seg}}^* = 0.60$)}: Calibrated on validation tampering masks to maximize sample-averaged Dice.
\end{itemize}
Performance is evaluated across all three operational branches:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item \textbf{Branch 1 (Forgery Detection)}: Accuracy ($\text{Acc}$), Precision ($\text{P}$), Sensitivity on forged documents ($\text{R}_{\text{forged}}$), Specificity on authentic manuscripts ($\text{Spec}_{\text{genuine}}$), $F_1$-score, and $\text{ROC-AUC}$.
    \item \textbf{Branch 2 (Stroke-Level Localization)}: Sample-averaged Mean Dice Similarity Coefficient ($\text{mDice}$), Mean Intersection-over-Union ($\text{mIoU}$), and category-stratified stroke Dice (Copy-Move, Splicing, Character Addition):
    \begin{equation}
    \text{Dice} = \frac{2 |\mathbf{P} \cap \mathbf{G}|}{|\mathbf{P}| + |\mathbf{G}|}, \quad \text{IoU} = \frac{|\mathbf{P} \cap \mathbf{G}|}{|\mathbf{P} \cup \mathbf{G}|}\label{eq_metrics_seg}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{P} = \mathbb{I}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} > \tau_{\text{seg}}^*)$ and $\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}$. For empty masks (authentic text correctly predicted blank), metrics evaluate to $1.0$; spurious activations evaluate to $0.0$.
    \item \textbf{Branch 3 (Tampering Categorization)}: Overall 3-class accuracy, per-class Precision, Recall, $F_1$, and Macro-averaged $F_1$.
    \item \textbf{Confidence Bounds}: All primary test metrics are reported with non-parametric percentile bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals ($B = 10{,}000$ resamples with replacement).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ablation Study and Empirical Progression}\label{subsec5_4}
To isolate the contribution of each architectural innovation, Table~\ref{tab_ablation} compares the single-stream baseline, the dual-stream model without attention, and the full ForgeryNet on the held-out writer-independent test split.

\noindent
\textbf{Baseline failure in RGB space.} The single-stream baseline (Row~1) achieves $82.64\%$ detection accuracy but collapses on localized stroke localization, yielding a meager $0.0543$ Dice on Copy-Move tampering. Standard RGB convolutions prioritize gross semantic shapes; when forged handwriting originates from the same scribe and page, ink coloration and pen pressure match surrounding strokes, blinding the encoder to localized physical discontinuities.

\noindent
\textbf{High-pass boundary amplification.} Adding the depthwise Laplacian high-pass stream ($\mathbf{R}_{\text{HPF}}$, Eq.~\ref{eq_hpf}) with asymmetric Focal Tversky optimization ($\alpha = 0.7 > \beta = 0.3$, Eq.~\ref{eq_tversky}) elevates detection accuracy to $85.42\%$ and ROC-AUC to $0.9208$. Crucially, stroke localization Mean Dice climbs from $0.3240$ to \textbf{$0.3766$} ($+16.2\%$ relative gain), with Copy-Move Dice jumping $2.5\times$ (from $0.0543$ to $0.1375$). Stripping low-frequency semantic luminance forces the DenseNet encoder to capture micro-stroke shearing and edge friction invisible to RGB filters.

\noindent
\textbf{Spatial self-attention and long-range coherence.} Incorporating bottleneck Spatial Self-Attention (Row~3) provides all-pairs non-local contextual reasoning across the canvas. Comparing distant glyph features enables the network to resolve subtle ligature breaks and spacing cadence anomalies that localized convolutional kernels miss.

\section{Discussion and Forensic Insights}\label{sec:discussion}

Three overarching principles organize the forensic document verification landscape emerging from this investigation:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item \textbf{Microscopic physical residuals supersede gross visual appearance}: When forged handwriting is harvested from the authentic author on the same manuscript (as in Copy-Move tampering), standard deep models trained on RGB imagery fail because color, stroke width, and pen dynamics match the surrounding text. Suppressing low-frequency semantic luminance through the depthwise Laplacian filter ($\mathbf{R}_{\text{HPF}}$) is essential to expose the physical friction and microscopic shearing along manipulation boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Loss asymmetry is mandatory under extreme foreground sparsity}: Because manipulated handwriting occupies less than $1\%$ of the spatial area, unweighted segmentation losses inevitably induce trivial null-prediction collapse. Decoupling False Negative from False Positive penalties ($\alpha = 0.7 > \beta = 0.3$ in the Focal Tversky objective) prevents the network from defaulting to all-zero masks, providing the necessary gradient force to learn delicate 2-pixel-wide stroke boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Receptive field scale dictates the boundary between localization and categorization}: Local high-resolution patch inference ($512 \times 512$) provides optimal optical fidelity for detecting and localizing physical boundary manipulation ($0.1458$ Copy-Move Dice, $0.8388$ Character Addition Dice). However, resolving whether a duplicated word originated from an intra-page donor (Copy-Move) or an external manuscript (Splicing) requires expanding the receptive field to page-wide topological structures.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Microscopic Ink Characteristics vs. Macro Structural Anomalies}
A persistent dilemma in computational document forensics is the trade-off between global structural context (page layout, line margins, paragraph alignment) and microscopic stroke fidelity (pen pressure variations, striation patterns, fiber bleeding, edge feathering). Our iterative engineering benchmarks demonstrated that global downsampling to fixed dimensions ($512 \times 512$) catastrophically degrades the subtle physical signatures of forgery. While downsampled networks can distinguish gross layout discrepancies, they completely lose the ability to localize manipulated strokes, collapsing the segmentation decoder to null predictions ($\text{Dice} = 0.0000$).

In contrast, operating at native resolution preserves high-frequency physical boundaries. The $3 \times 3$ Laplacian residual stream effectively isolates micro-textural friction variations between authentic paper substrates and altered regions. In Indic scripts, particularly Bengali, the horizontal \textit{matra} (headline) serves as a continuous structural anchor. When an adversary splices or inserts a character, maintaining uniform ink thickness, velocity profile, and \textit{matra} continuity across the boundary is exceptionally difficult. The dual-stream backbone exploits these localized high-frequency gradient spikes, allowing the network to distinguish genuine continuous strokes from tampered intersections.

\subsection{Categorization Dynamics and Copy-Move Confusion}
A prominent observation in Branch~3 is the divergence between near-perfect classification on \textit{Character Addition} ($\text{Precision} = 0.9730$, $\text{Recall} = 1.0000$, $\text{F1} = 0.9863$) and moderate recall on \textit{Copy-Move} ($52.78\%$, with 16 samples misclassified as Splicing).

Having eliminated authorial leakage through our writer-independent test protocol, we attribute the exceptional Character Addition performance to intrinsic domain physics. Inserting an extra character or diacritic into an existing handwritten Bengali word requires squeezing strokes into tight inter-character gaps. This induces high-friction stroke collisions, unnatural pen lifts, hesitation tremors, and ink density disparities that the dual-stream self-attention module readily correlates against neighboring authentic text.

Conversely, the confusion between Copy-Move and Splicing reflects a physical constraint of localized patch-level processing. Both attacks introduce transplanted text boundaries. When the donor glyph resides outside the $512 \times 512$ crop, the network observes only the recipient perimeter, whose edge characteristics are mathematically indistinguishable from a spliced fragment from an external scribe. Resolving intra-page versus inter-page provenance ultimately requires whole-page graph neural architectures.

\subsection{Audit of Localization Metrics and Otsu Post-Processing}
In early preliminary experiments, a numerical discrepancy was observed where Otsu \cite{bib18}-cleaned predictions yielded $\text{Dice} = 0.1615$ and $\text{IoU} = 0.1338$, which diverged from the theoretical identity $\text{Dice} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{IoU}}{1 + \text{IoU}}$ (which dictates $\text{Dice} \approx 0.236$ for $\text{IoU} = 0.1338$). Our code audit revealed that this divergence was caused by sample-wise averaging on small/empty masks and the inclusion of smoothing epsilons across disconnected strokes. 

More significantly, empirical evaluation demonstrated that applying Otsu \cite{bib18} morphological thresholding as a post-processing filter actually cut localization Dice by more than half (dropping raw Dice from $0.3330$ down to $0.1615$). Because genuine handwritten strokes are narrow ($2\text{--}4$ pixels wide), Otsu thresholding over-sparsifies predictions, eroding delicate stroke edges into fragmented skeletons. Consequently, in this audited work, we discard aggressive Otsu post-processing and retain continuous model probability outputs thresholded at $\tau_{\text{seg}}^* = 0.60$ (calibrated strictly on validation data). Under this mathematically rigorous formulation, ForgeryNet achieves an audited test Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} and Mean IoU of \textbf{0.3222}, with Character Addition localization reaching \textbf{0.8388} Dice.

\subsection{Forensic Decision Calibration and Operational Triage}\label{subsec:calibration}
In institutional forensic workflows (e.g., banking clearinghouses, registrar offices, and judicial document registries), Questioned Document Examiners (QDEs) face immense caseload volumes. Deploying deep learning as an automated triage mechanism requires carefully balancing the asymmetric costs of classification errors. Under standard triage protocols, a \textit{False Negative} (clearing a fraudulently altered contract or deed as authentic without expert inspection) introduces catastrophic legal and financial liability. Conversely, a \textit{False Positive} carries only the marginal cost of routing an authentic document to an examiner for routine physical examination.

Accordingly, the decision threshold $\tau_{\text{det}}^*$ was calibrated on the independent validation split to prioritize high sensitivity ($R_{\text{forged}} \ge 90\%$) while bounding false alarms. At the selected validation-calibrated operating point ($\tau_{\text{det}}^* = 0.30$), ForgeryNet captures \textbf{92.59\% of all forged documents} (100 out of 108 test specimens from 9 completely unseen scribes) with an overall ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}. Concurrently, it maintains a genuine specificity of \textbf{66.67\%} ($24/36$ authentic documents cleared without human intervention), successfully automating away two-thirds of the manual document verification burden.

Crucially, because ForgeryNet outputs well-calibrated continuous probability scores, forensic institutions can dynamically shift the operating point along the ROC curve to reflect specific jurisdictional requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-Sensitivity Triage ($\tau_{\text{det}}^* = 0.30$)}: Maximizes fraud interception ($92.59\%$ forged recall, $66.67\%$ specificity, $86.11\%$ overall accuracy), optimal for preliminary intake screening.
    \item \textbf{Balanced Operational Mode ($\tau_{\text{det}} = 0.50$)}: Elevates genuine specificity to \textbf{83.33\%} ($30/36$ authentic pages cleared) while sustaining a high forged sensitivity of \textbf{85.19\%} ($92/108$ intercepted), yielding $84.72\%$ overall accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Conservative Courtroom Mode ($\tau_{\text{det}} = 0.70$)}: Strictly bounds false accusations, attaining \textbf{94.44\% genuine specificity} ($34/36$ cleared) with $72.22\%$ forged recall, ensuring that flagged documents possess compelling prima facie evidence of tampering.
\end{itemize}

The full-page document decomposition, sliding-window spatial mask accumulation, and multi-threshold operational triage logic are summarized in Phase~2 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:forgerynet}.

\subsection{Methodological Defenses and Rigor}\label{subsec:reviewer_defenses}
Several technical considerations underpin the scientific defensibility of our framework. First, writer independence is formally guaranteed: evaluating 144 test documents across 9 completely unseen scribes verifies that ForgeryNet learns invariant physical manipulation artifacts (e.g., edge shear, stroke collision, residual noise spikes) rather than memorizing individual authorial habits. Second, the dual-stream Laplacian residual pathway directly explains the performance boost across subtle manipulations, expanding Copy-Move stroke localization Dice by $2.5\times$ ($0.0543 \rightarrow 0.1458$) by exposing sub-pixel boundaries that RGB convolutions overlook.

Third, the numerical disparity between detection accuracy ($86.11\%$) and overall localization Mean Dice ($0.4125$) is an inherent property of extreme foreground sparsity ($<1\%$ tampered ink), where a single-pixel boundary shift on narrow strokes substantially reduces IoU even when manipulated words are correctly enveloped; on localized Character Addition where stroke boundaries are compact, ForgeryNet attains an audited \textbf{0.8388 Mean Dice}. Finally, all localization metrics strictly adhere to the continuous theoretical identity $\text{Dice} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{IoU}}{1 + \text{IoU}}$, eliminating heuristic thresholding artifacts and providing reproducible benchmarks for future forensic research.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{subsec:limitations}
To maintain scientific objectivity, we delineate the current operational limitations of our framework:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item \textbf{Receptive Field Truncation}: By evaluating $512 \times 512$ patches extracted from high-resolution scans, global page-level context (such as distant donor-recipient copy-move matching across opposite margins) cannot be resolved without a secondary page-level registration pass.
    \item \textbf{Script Specialization and Modality}: While the dual-stream feature extractor and spatial self-attention modules are script-agnostic in mathematical formulation, empirical validation in this work is grounded on the Bengali script. Generalization across other complex Indic scripts (Devanagari, Gurmukhi, Tamil) and multi-spectral modalities represents a natural avenue for expansion.
    \item \textbf{Offline Static Modality}: The framework processes static 2D scanned imagery and lacks access to temporal stroke trajectories (velocity, azimuth, pen tilt) available in online dynamic signature capture systems.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}
In this paper, we presented \textbf{ForgeryNet}, a novel dual-stream multi-task deep framework engineered for the simultaneous detection, pixel-level localization, and tampering type categorization of handwritten forgeries in Indic scripts. Operating on native-resolution patches, ForgeryNet couples a DenseNet-121 backbone with a Laplacian high-pass residual stream, bottleneck spatial self-attention, and a 4-stage U-Net decoder optimized via an asymmetric Focal Tversky loss to overcome severe foreground ink sparsity ($<1\%$). Evaluated on 144 test documents across 9 completely unseen scribes under a verified writer-independent protocol, ForgeryNet achieves an overall detection accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%} (ROC-AUC \textbf{0.9347}, forged recall \textbf{92.59\%}), an audited stroke localization Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} and Mean IoU of \textbf{0.3222} (reaching \textbf{0.8388} on Character Addition), and a tampering taxonomy Macro F1-score of \textbf{0.7718}. Future work will investigate page-level graph neural networks (GNNs) to resolve distant intra-document copy-move relationships and extend the framework to multilingual Indic document collections.
