\begin{abstract}
Digital verification of handwritten Indic documents presents acute forensic challenges due to cursive orthography, continuous headlines (\textit{matra}), and high morphological intra-writer variability. Conventional forensic architectures primarily examine Latin scripts and evaluate forgery detection, localization, and tampering categorization as isolated tasks. We propose \textbf{ForgeryNet}, a unified multi-task deep framework to concurrently detect, localize, and classify handwritten Bengali document tampering. ForgeryNet couples a DenseNet-121 backbone with a Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor (combining native RGB representations with a depthwise Laplacian high-frequency edge-discontinuity stream) and a bottleneck Spatial Self-Attention module. The architecture concurrently branches into: (i) an anomaly-calibrated binary detector, (ii) a 4-stage U-Net segmentation decoder optimized via an asymmetric Focal Tversky and stroke-weighted objective to overcome extreme pixel sparsity ($<1\%$ tampered ink), and (iii) a masked 3-way taxonomy classifier separating Copy-Move, Splicing, and Character Addition. The framework operates on high-resolution $512 \times 512$ patches extracted at native scanner fidelity under a verified writer-independent protocol across 55 scribes. Evaluated on an audited held-out test split of 144 Bengali document patches from 9 completely unseen writers, ForgeryNet achieves an overall detection accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%}, an ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}, and a forged document sensitivity of \textbf{92.59\%} at the validation-calibrated operating point. In stroke-level localization, the framework attains a sample-wise Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} and Mean IoU of \textbf{0.3222} (Character Addition reaching \textbf{0.8388} Dice), substantially outperforming standard single-stream baselines (\textbf{0.3240} Dice). In tampering categorization, it attains a Macro F1-score of \textbf{0.7718}, correctly identifying all 36 Character Addition test instances ($1.00$ recall, $0.9863$ F1). Multi-task feature sharing combined with high-frequency residuals and spatial attention significantly improves detection reliability and localization viability for complex, non-Latin handwritten scripts without authorial leakage.

\keywords{Handwritten Document Forensics \and Indic Scripts \and Multi-Task Learning \and Forgery Localization \and Bengali Script \and Writer-Independent Evaluation.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Handwritten documents remain foundational legal, financial, and administrative artifacts worldwide, serving as legal contracts, land property deeds, banking instruments, and judicial evidence. However, the accessibility of high-resolution scanning hardware and consumer-grade image editing suites has drastically lowered the barrier for malicious document tampering. Fraudulent alterations---predominantly spanning \textit{Copy-Move} (duplicating legitimate words or signatures across the document), \textit{Splicing} (transplanting foreign strokes or words from external manuscripts), and \textit{Character Addition} (inserting discrete strokes, diacritics, or modifiers to fundamentally alter financial figures or semantic intent)---inflict severe economic, legal, and institutional harm.

Automated digital document forensics has consequently emerged as an active area of research within document analysis and recognition. While digital forgery detection in natural scenes has advanced rapidly, the forensic analysis of \textbf{handwritten documents} introduces four distinct, domain-specific obstacles:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item \textbf{Extreme Spatial Sparsity}: Unlike natural image manipulations (e.g., object removal or splicing) that span extensive connected pixel manifolds ($20\%-50\%$ of the canvas), handwritten document tampering typically alters only a single numeral, diacritic, or word. Consequently, forged pixels comprise less than $1\%$ of a high-resolution page, causing standard semantic segmentation models to suffer from gradient starvation and collapse into trivial all-background predictions ($\text{Dice} = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Orthographic Complexity of Indic Scripts}: Bengali, the seventh most spoken language globally, exhibits complex cursive morphology characterized by a continuous top-hanging horizontal headline (\textit{matra}), intricate consonant conjuncts (\textit{juktakkhor}), and floating diacritical markers. Fine variations in stroke velocity, localized pen pressure, and ink bleeding are difficult to separate from deliberate manual tampering.
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Methodological Paradigms}: The majority of existing forensic architectures address document authentication through fragmented pipelines: training separate single-task models for coarse document-level binary classification or independent pixel segmentation, entirely omitting the underlying forensic taxonomy (identifying whether a detected tampering corresponds to copy-move, splicing, or character addition).
    \item \textbf{Risk of Writer Leakage in Forensic Evaluation}: Handwriting style exhibits high intra-author consistency and pronounced inter-author variability across stroke curvature, slant, and ligature formation. When document splits are performed at random across samples rather than partitioned strictly by writer identity, deep networks exploit familiar individual handwriting quirks rather than invariant forensic tampering cues, severely inflating reported performance.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome these structural limitations, we propose \textbf{ForgeryNet}, a unified, multi-task deep learning architecture formulated specifically for handwritten Indic document verification. ForgeryNet processes high-resolution scans and concurrently resolves three interrelated forensic objectives: (i) an anomaly-calibrated binary detector (Branch 1), (ii) a dense stroke-level localization decoder (Branch 2), and (iii) a masked 3-way taxonomy classifier separating Copy-Move, Splicing, and Character Addition (Branch 3).

To preserve fragile microscopic forensic artifacts---such as paper texture erasure, compression boundary artifacts, and edge blending---ForgeryNet introduces a \textbf{Dual-Stream Forensic Extractor} that pairs native RGB representations with a depthwise Laplacian high-pass residual filter. Furthermore, a numerically stable \textbf{Spatial Self-Attention} module is embedded at the bottleneck to perform all-pairs spatial feature comparisons, enabling the network to resolve long-range duplicated patterns characteristic of copy-move manipulations. Finally, by operating on a \textbf{Native-Resolution Patch Pipeline} and optimizing under a composite \textbf{Focal Tversky Loss}, the proposed framework resolves the background-collapse dilemma on sparse handwriting.

\noindent
The primary contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[1.]
    \item We design and implement \textbf{ForgeryNet}, an end-to-end multi-task deep neural framework capable of simultaneous detection, stroke-level localization, and 3-way taxonomy classification for handwritten Indic documents.
    \item We incorporate a dual-stream high-frequency noise residual pathway and bottleneck spatial self-attention, significantly enhancing the network's discriminative sensitivity to micro-edge anomalies and long-range text duplication.
    \item We formulate an asymmetric Focal Tversky and positive-weighted objective function tailored for extreme class imbalance ($<1\%$ tampered ink), preventing trivial zero-mask convergence.
    \item We enforce a strict, verified \textbf{writer-independent evaluation protocol} across 55 scribes, eliminating writer leakage. On 144 test documents from 9 completely unseen writers, ForgeryNet achieves an overall detection accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%}, an ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}, a forged sensitivity of \textbf{92.59\%}, a localization Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} (a $+27.3\%$ improvement over standard baselines), and a taxonomy Macro F1-score of \textbf{0.7718}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec2}

Document forgery analysis has evolved from handcrafted feature engineering to modern deep representation learning. In this section, we review relevant literature across classical document forensics, deep learning-based image manipulation detection, and Indic handwritten scripts.

\subsection{Classical Handcrafted Forensic Methods}\label{subsec2_1}
Early document authentication methods predominantly relied on handcrafted feature descriptors aimed at detecting statistical anomalies introduced during manipulation \cite{bib1}. For copy-move detection, block-based matching techniques utilizing Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and Zernike moments were widely adopted to identify duplicated pixel blocks \cite{bib1}. Keypoint-based approaches, notably Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) and Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF), were introduced to achieve invariance against geometric transformations such as scaling and rotation.

In document-specific scenarios, researchers explored color texture descriptors, ink-deposition profiling, and illumination inconsistencies. For example, local binary patterns (LBP) and steerable pyramids were employed to capture scanner noise patterns and halftone screen irregularities \cite{bib1}. However, handcrafted methods exhibit acute vulnerabilities when applied to handwritten documents: cursive handwriting lacks the rigid corner features required by SIFT/SURF, and localized pen-pressure variations frequently generate false positive keypoint correspondences. Furthermore, classical approaches are computationally prohibitive on large high-resolution scans and fail to generalize across diverse handwriting styles.

\subsection{Deep Learning for Image and Document Forensics}\label{subsec2_2}
The advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) has revolutionized forensic imaging. Early deep architectures repurposed general-purpose image classification networks (e.g., VGG, ResNet) for binary patch classification \cite{bib4}, while hybrid architectures integrated convolutional features with recurrent sequential models to trace spatial tampering dependencies \cite{bib5}. Concurrently, camera-model fingerprints and noise residuals, such as Noiseprint \cite{bib3}, established that sensor-level inconsistencies provide vital forensic cues.

To suppress semantic dominance and force networks to focus on forensic anomalies, researchers introduced constrained convolutional layers and noise residual streams. Bayar and Stamm \cite{bib7} proposed an adaptive constrained convolution layer that suppresses image content while learning spatial manipulation residuals. Spatial Rich Models (SRM) \cite{bib8}, originating from digital steganography, demonstrated that high-pass filtering exposes interpolation artifacts and localized resampling discrepancies. In natural image tampering localization, architectures such as ManTra-Net \cite{bib9} and BusterNet \cite{bib10} introduced dual-branch feature extractors combining visual appearance with noise features. Nevertheless, these networks are calibrated for natural images containing expansive tampered objects and degrade severely when applied to fine-grained handwritten text where ink strokes occupy less than $1\%$ of the total pixel volume.

\subsection{Indic Script Processing and Handwriting Verification}\label{subsec2_3}
Research targeting handwritten Indic scripts has historically focused on Optical Character Recognition (OCR), writer identification, and offline signature verification \cite{bib2,bib11,bib12}. The structural characteristics of Bengali script---specifically the continuous horizontal headline (\textit{matra}), complex consonant-vowel ligatures, and dynamic baseline variations---render automated segmentation and forensic analysis particularly challenging.

While several offline signature verification benchmarks exist (e.g., GPDS, CEDAR, and BHSig260 \cite{bib13}), they evaluate global ballistic trajectories rather than localized content manipulations (such as character alterations or sentence-level insertions) embedded within authentic text bodies. More critically, dedicated pixel-level tampering benchmarks for continuous Indic handwritten scripts remain virtually absent in the scientific literature. To bridge this fundamental void, this work introduces a newly developed, ground-up Bengali Handwriting Forgery Benchmark alongside a unified multi-task deep architecture engineered to concurrently handle detection, pixel-level stroke localization, and 3-way taxonomy classification tailored specifically for handwritten Indic documents.

\section{Dataset and Problem Formulation}\label{sec3}

\subsection{Benchmark Construction and Operational Categories}\label{subsec3_1}
While digital image forgery detection has advanced rapidly for natural photographic imagery and Latin-script typography, forensic datasets dedicated to \textbf{Indic handwriting manipulation} are virtually non-existent in the public research domain. Indic scripts---and Bengali in particular---feature distinctive orthographic structures that fundamentally depart from Western Latin characters: (i) an unbroken horizontal headline (\textit{matra}) establishing an interconnected stroke baseline across adjacent letters, (ii) complex consonant conjuncts (\textit{juktakkhors}) fusing multiple glyphs into non-linear ligatures, and (iii) multi-tier vowel diacritics (\textit{kar} signs) floating above, below, or flanking the glyph body. In practical administrative, judicial, and financial records, document tampering in Indic handwriting often manifests as micro-stroke modifications---such as altering a single diacritic, extending a \textit{matra}, or inserting a localized character to falsify monetary amounts, dates, or legal clauses.

To address this absence of accessible Indic handwriting forensics resources, we constructed the \textbf{Bengali Handwriting Forgery Benchmark} from the ground up:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authentic Data Collection}: Genuine handwritten manuscript pages were systematically collected from 55 distinct native Bengali scribes writing unconstrained administrative, financial, and legal prose on standard physical paper under diverse pen instruments (ballpoint, gel, ink) and ambient conditions.
    \item \textbf{Expert-Controlled Manipulation Synthesis}: Human operators systematically executed three realistic tampering techniques directly on the genuine manuscripts:
    \begin{enumerate}[1.]
        \item \textit{Copy-Move Forgeries}: Legitimate characters, numerals, or words were cloned from one section of a scribe's manuscript and duplicated elsewhere on the same page. Because donor and target text originate from the identical document, the paper fiber texture, ink chemistry, and authorial pen pressure remain authentic.
        \item \textit{Splicing Forgeries}: Words or clauses harvested from manuscripts of external scribes were digitally transplanted into the target page, introducing subtle inter-document illumination differences, microscopic edge gradients, and scanner noise discrepancies.
        \item \textit{Character Addition}: Discrete individual characters, digits, or diacritical modifiers were micro-inserted into existing words to alter financial figures or semantic intent.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pixel-Accurate Ground-Truth Annotation}: For every manipulated document, a stroke-accurate binary ground-truth mask $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}} \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$ was manually traced and verified at 100\% native optical resolution ($1$ denotes manipulated ink strokes; $0$ denotes authentic handwriting or paper background). Authentic manuscripts are paired with synthetic null masks ($\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}} = \mathbf{0}$) to penalize false-positive activations. Representative document patches and their corresponding ground-truth localization masks across all four operational categories are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig_dataset_samples}.
\end{itemize}

The finalized benchmark comprises 880 high-resolution document scans preserved in uncompressed tagged image file format (\texttt{.tif}) at full optical scanner resolution ($\approx 2000 \times 1500$ pixels), balanced uniformly across four operational categories (220 document images per category: Genuine, Copy-Move, Splicing, and Character Addition). With 880 full documents across 55 scribes, this benchmark exceeds standard forensics corpora in scale and diversity (e.g., CoMoFoD with 260 images; COVERAGE with 100 images).

\subsection{Writer-Independent Partitioning}\label{subsec3_2}
To prevent \textbf{writer leakage}---where networks memorize author-specific quirks rather than invariant manipulation artifacts---we enforce a strict \textbf{writer-independent disjoint split} across all 55 native Bengali scribes. Each scribe contributes exactly 16 documents (4 genuine and 4 in each of the three tampering categories). We partition the scribes deterministically into three mutually exclusive subsets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Set (38 Writers, 69.5\%)}: 612 documents (153 Genuine, 153 Copy-Move, 153 Splicing, 153 Character Addition).
    \item \textbf{Validation Set (8 Writers, 14.1\%)}: 124 documents (31 per category).
    \item \textbf{Testing Set (9 Writers, 16.4\%)}: 144 documents (36 Genuine, 36 Copy-Move, 36 Splicing, 36 Character Addition).
\end{itemize}
The writer subsets satisfy strict mutual disjointness: $\mathcal{W}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{W}_{\text{val}} = \emptyset$, $\mathcal{W}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{W}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$, and $\mathcal{W}_{\text{val}} \cap \mathcal{W}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$. All patches derived from a given document strictly inherit the author's partition. The complete dataset distribution is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_split}.

\subsection{Native-Resolution Patch Formulation}\label{subsec3_3}
Standard vision architectures downsample document scans directly to $256 \times 256$ or $512 \times 512$, squashing delicate 2-pixel-wide handwriting strokes into unresolved sub-pixel blur and destroying microscopic edge-blending cues. To preserve physical fidelity, we implement a \textbf{Native-Resolution Patch Formulation}. High-resolution patches $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 512 \times 512}$ are extracted directly at $100\%$ native scanner fidelity. During training, patch extraction is strategically biased: for tampered documents, $70\%$ of crops are centered on coordinates where $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}(i, j) = 1$, ensuring an information-dense stream of active manipulation boundaries across epochs, while authentic document crops are sampled uniformly.

\subsection{Formal Multi-Task Problem Definition}\label{subsec3_4}
Given an input patch $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ ($H = W = 512$), the objective is to learn a joint mapping parameterized by weights $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{X}; \boldsymbol{\Theta}) \longrightarrow \{\hat{y}_{\text{det}}, \, \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}, \, \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{type}}\}\label{eq_problem}
\end{equation}
subject to three simultaneous tasks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task 1: Forgery Detection (Branch 1)}: Compute a scalar probability $\hat{y}_{\text{det}} \in [0, 1]$ indicating whether the patch contains an authentic ($y_{\text{bin}} = 0$) or manipulated ($y_{\text{bin}} = 1$) text region.
    \item \textbf{Task 2: Stroke-Level Localization (Branch 2)}: Generate a dense 2D probability mask $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$, where activated pixels delineate the physical contours of manipulated strokes.
    \item \textbf{Task 3: Tampering Categorization (Branch 3)}: Predict a categorical probability vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{type}} \in \Delta^2$ over the 3-simplex spanning $\{\text{Copy-Move}, \text{Splicing}, \text{Character Addition}\}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Methodology: ForgeryNet}\label{sec4}

The architectural framework of \textbf{ForgeryNet} is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig_arch}. It integrates four core components: (i) a Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor, (ii) a DenseNet-121 hierarchical backbone, (iii) a bottleneck Spatial Self-Attention module, and (iv) three specialized multi-task prediction heads optimized under a composite objective.

\subsection{Dual-Stream Forensic Residual Extractor}\label{subsec4_1}
Authentic handwriting is governed by continuous physical ink absorption, natural pen pressure dynamics, and consistent scanner sensor noise. Manipulating a document disrupts this baseline consistency, introducing high-frequency edge gradients, interpolation artifacts, and localized compression anomalies that are often imperceptible in RGB. Standard CNNs trained exclusively on RGB imagery tend to prioritize high-level semantic shapes while suppressing low-level forensic anomalies.

To force the feature extractor to evaluate forensic discrepancies, ForgeryNet introduces a parallel high-frequency residual stream. We deploy a fixed depthwise Laplacian High-Pass Filter (HPF) kernel $\mathbf{K}_{\text{HPF}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 1 \times 3 \times 3}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{K}_{\text{HPF}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 8 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\label{eq_hpf}
\end{equation}
Given an input patch $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$, the high-frequency residual map $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ is computed via channel-wise depthwise convolution followed by a sigmoid non-linearity:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{X} * \mathbf{K}_{\text{HPF}}\right)\label{eq_residual}
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes depthwise spatial convolution and $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$. The visual appearance stream $\mathbf{X}$ and the high-frequency residual stream $\mathbf{R}$ are concatenated along the channel dimension to form a 6-channel composite tensor:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\text{dual}} = [\mathbf{X} \,\|\, \mathbf{R}] \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times H \times W}\label{eq_concat}
\end{equation}
The initial convolutional layer of the backbone ($\text{conv0}$) is modified from 3 to 6 input channels, with the new residual weights initialized from ImageNet-pretrained RGB filters.

\subsection{DenseNet-121 Hierarchical Backbone}\label{subsec4_2}
We employ DenseNet-121 \cite{bib14} as the foundational feature extractor. Direct feed-forward connections from every layer to all subsequent layers within a dense block:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_\ell = H_\ell([\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}])\label{eq_dense}
\end{equation}
preserve fine-grained, high-frequency boundary gradients across downsampling stages, preventing delicate ink stroke artifacts from vanishing in deeper layers. The encoder produces five progressive spatial representations:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{s}_1 &\in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times \frac{H}{2} \times \frac{W}{2}}, \quad \mathbf{s}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times \frac{H}{4} \times \frac{W}{4}}, \quad \mathbf{s}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times \frac{H}{8} \times \frac{W}{8}}, \nonumber \\
\mathbf{s}_4 &\in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times \frac{H}{16} \times \frac{W}{16}}, \quad \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times \frac{H}{32} \times \frac{W}{32}}\label{eq_features}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{s}_5$ represents the deepest bottleneck feature map ($16 \times 16$ spatial resolution for an input patch of $512 \times 512$).

\subsection{Spatial Self-Attention Bottleneck}\label{subsec4_3}
Standard convolutions possess localized receptive fields, limiting the network's ability to correlate spatially distant text regions---a capability critical for detecting Copy-Move forgeries where cloned text is replicated across separate lines. To establish non-local feature correspondences, we incorporate a Spatial Self-Attention module \cite{bib15} operating over the bottleneck features $\mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times h \times w}$ ($C = 1024, h = w = 16$).

The feature map is linearly projected into Query ($\mathbf{Q}$), Key ($\mathbf{K}$), and Value ($\mathbf{V}$) spaces using $1 \times 1$ convolutions: $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C' \times N}$, $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C' \times N}$, $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{s}_5 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}$, where $C' = C / 8 = 128$ and $N = h \cdot w = 256$. The attention matrix is computed in FP32 with numerical clamping $[-50, 50]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_{i, j} = \frac{\exp\left(\text{clamp}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_i^T \mathbf{K}_j}{\sqrt{C'}}, -50, 50\right)\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^N \exp\left(\text{clamp}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_i^T \mathbf{K}_k}{\sqrt{C'}}, -50, 50\right)\right)}\label{eq_attn_matrix}
\end{equation}
The attention-enhanced feature representation $\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}$ is obtained via residual addition:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}} = \gamma \cdot (\mathbf{V} \mathbf{A}) + \mathbf{s}_5\label{eq_attn_res}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is a learnable scalar initialized to zero, allowing the network to gradually integrate non-local context without disrupting pretrained encoder representations.

\subsection{Multi-Task Output Heads}\label{subsec4_4}
The representation $\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}$ branches into three specialized heads:

\subsubsection{Branch 1: Binary Forgery Detection Head}
Global Average Pooling (GAP) reduces $\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}$ to a 1024-dimensional vector, followed by a dense classifier ($\text{Linear}_{1024 \rightarrow 256} \rightarrow \text{ReLU} \rightarrow \text{Dropout}_{0.5} \rightarrow \text{Linear}_{256 \rightarrow 1}$) producing scalar logit $\hat{y}_{\text{det}}$.

\subsubsection{Branch 2: Stroke-Level Localization Decoder}
To recover fine stroke details lost during pooling, we construct a 4-stage U-Net style decoder with skip connections \cite{bib6}. The bottleneck features pass through a convolutional bridge and four successive upsampling blocks ($\text{UpBlock}_4$ to $\text{UpBlock}_1$):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_k = \text{ConvBlock}\Big(\big[\text{ConvTrans}(\mathbf{d}_{k+1}) \,\|\, \mathbf{s}_k\big]\Big)\label{eq_upblock}
\end{equation}
Each stage doubles spatial resolution using $2 \times 2$ transposed convolutions, concatenates with encoder skip features ($\mathbf{s}_4, \mathbf{s}_3, \mathbf{s}_2, \mathbf{s}_1$), and applies dual $3 \times 3$ convolutions with Batch Normalization. The final stage produces the full-resolution probability mask:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} = \sigma\left(\text{Conv}_{1 \times 1}\big(\text{ReLU}(\text{ConvTrans}_{64 \rightarrow 32}(\mathbf{d}_1))\big)\right)\label{eq_seg_head}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$.

\subsubsection{Branch 3: Tampering Categorization Head}
Tampering taxonomy classification relies on both global document characteristics and local decoder bridge representations:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{type}} = [\text{GAP}(\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}) \,\|\, \text{GAP}(\text{Bridge}(\mathbf{s}_5^{\text{att}}))] \in \mathbb{R}^{1536}\label{eq_type_feat}
\end{equation}
The composite vector $\mathbf{f}_{\text{type}}$ passes through a dense classifier ($\text{Linear}_{1536 \rightarrow 512} \rightarrow \text{ReLU} \rightarrow \text{Dropout}_{0.5} \rightarrow \text{Linear}_{512 \rightarrow 3}$) to output class logits $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{type}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

\subsection{Multi-Task Loss Formulation}\label{subsec4_5}
The network is optimized end-to-end via a composite objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \lambda_{\text{det}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} + \lambda_{\text{loc}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}} + \lambda_{\text{type}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{type}}\label{eq_loss_total}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{det}} = 1.0$, $\lambda_{\text{loc}} = 4.0$, and $\lambda_{\text{type}} = 1.0$.

\subsubsection{Balanced Detection Objective}
With $220$ Genuine vs. $660$ Forged documents, positive loss weighting restores decision balance:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} = - \Big[ w_{\text{det}} \cdot y_{\text{bin}} \log(\sigma(\hat{y}_{\text{det}})) + (1 - y_{\text{bin}}) \log(1 - \sigma(\hat{y}_{\text{det}})) \Big]\label{eq_loss_det}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\text{det}} = 220 / 660 \approx 0.33$, centering the decision threshold around $0.50$.

\subsubsection{Focal Tversky Localization Objective}
Because manipulated handwriting strokes occupy less than $1\%$ of the spatial pixels, unweighted segmentation losses suffer from severe background dominance, causing models to predict trivial all-zero masks. We formulate a composite localization objective combining positive-weighted BCE with Focal Tversky Loss (FTL) \cite{bib16,bib17}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}} = 0.5 \cdot \text{BCE}_{\text{pos}}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}; w_{\text{loc}}) + 0.5 \cdot \text{FTL}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}, \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}})\label{eq_loss_loc}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\text{loc}} = 15.0$ penalizes false negatives on ink pixels. The Tversky Index ($TI$) decouples penalties for False Negatives ($FN$) and False Positives ($FP$):
\begin{equation}
TI = \frac{\sum_i p_i g_i + \epsilon}{\sum_i p_i g_i + \alpha \sum_i (1 - p_i) g_i + \beta \sum_i p_i (1 - g_i) + \epsilon}\label{eq_tversky}
\end{equation}
where $p_i \in \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\text{loc}}$ and $g_i \in \mathbf{Y}_{\text{mask}}$ denote predicted and ground-truth pixel values, and $\epsilon = 1.0$. We set $\alpha = 0.7$ and $\beta = 0.3$. By configuring $\alpha > \beta$, missed tampered strokes are penalized over twice as heavily as background false alarms, providing the gradient force required to trace delicate 2-pixel-wide stroke boundaries. The Focal Tversky Loss introduces a focusing parameter $\gamma = 1.33$ to emphasize hard-to-detect ink strokes:
\begin{equation}
\text{FTL} = (1 - TI)^\gamma\label{eq_ftl}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Masked Categorical Cross-Entropy}
Authentic documents ($y_{\text{bin}} = 0$) do not possess a tampering type ($y_{\text{type}} = -1$). To prevent authentic samples from propagating destabilizing gradients into the 3-way classification head, we apply an indicator mask:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{type}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^B \mathbf{1}_{\{y_{\text{bin}}^{(i)} = 1\}} \cdot \text{CE}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{type}}^{(i)}, y_{\text{type}}^{(i)})}{\sum_{i=1}^B \mathbf{1}_{\{y_{\text{bin}}^{(i)} = 1\}} + \epsilon}\label{eq_loss_type}
\end{equation}
ensuring that only actively manipulated patches contribute to taxonomy optimization.

\section{Experimental Evaluation and Results}\label{sec5}

\subsection{Implementation and Setup}\label{subsec5_1}
ForgeryNet was implemented in PyTorch and trained on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24\,GB VRAM). Optimization utilized AdamW with weight decay $10^{-4}$, initial learning rate $10^{-4}$ scheduled via cosine annealing, batch size $8$, and automatic mixed precision (AMP FP16). Data augmentations included random horizontal flips ($p=0.5$), affine spatial transforms (rotation $\pm 10^\circ$, scaling $0.9\text{--}1.1$), brightness/contrast jitter ($\pm 15\%$), and additive Gaussian noise ($\sigma \le 0.02$).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Decision Calibration}\label{subsec5_2}
Performance is evaluated across all three tasks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detection (Branch 1)}: Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under Curve (ROC-AUC).
    \item \textbf{Localization (Branch 2)}: Sample-wise Mean Dice (F1), Mean Intersection over Union (IoU), Pixel Precision, and Pixel Recall computed at native resolution.
    \item \textbf{Categorization (Branch 3)}: Per-class Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Macro F1 across Copy-Move, Splicing, and Character Addition.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Audited Continuous Thresholding vs. Otsu Filtering}: In preliminary experiments, applying Otsu thresholding \cite{bib18} as an automated post-processing filter degraded stroke localization drastically (cutting Dice from $0.3330$ to $0.1615$). Because genuine handwritten strokes are narrow ($2\text{--}4$ pixels wide), Otsu thresholding over-sparsifies predictions, eroding delicate stroke edges into fragmented skeletons. Consequently, we discard aggressive Otsu filtering and retain continuous model probability outputs thresholded at $\tau_{\text{seg}}^* = 0.60$ (calibrated strictly on validation data). Under this formulation, metrics adhere to the theoretical identity $\text{Dice} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{IoU}}{1 + \text{IoU}}$.

\noindent
\textbf{Forensic Decision Calibration}: In forensic applications, falsely accusing authentic records carries severe legal risk. By tuning the detection threshold to $\tau_{\text{det}}^* = 0.30$ strictly on validation precision-recall curves, ForgeryNet intercepts \textbf{92.59\% of all forged specimens} ($100$ of $108$ forged test documents from unseen scribes) with \textbf{66.67\% genuine specificity}, yielding an overall accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%} and an ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}.

\subsection{Ablation Study and Empirical Progression}\label{subsec5_3}
To rigorously quantify the contribution of each architectural innovation, we conducted a progressive ablation study on the held-out writer-independent test set (144 documents from 9 unseen writers). As summarized in Table~\ref{tab_ablation}, each module provides distinct, quantifiable gains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline (RGB DenseNet-121)}: Achieves $79.86\%$ detection accuracy, $0.8872$ ROC-AUC, and $0.3240$ Mean Dice. However, Copy-Move localization remains severely impaired ($0.0543$ Dice), reflecting the limitation of RGB convolutions on identical-ink tampering.
    \item \textbf{+ Dual-Stream HPF Residual Stream}: Adding the depthwise Laplacian high-pass filter expands localization Mean Dice from $0.3240$ to $0.3766$ ($+16.2\%$ relative gain) and elevates Copy-Move localization Dice by $2.5\times$ ($0.0543 \rightarrow 0.1375$). The high-pass residual stream suppresses dominant textual semantics, exposing microscopic edge shearing and substrate friction disparities.
    \item \textbf{+ Spatial Self-Attention}: Incorporating bottleneck self-attention establishes long-range spatial correspondences, increasing detection accuracy to $84.03\%$ and boosting Copy-Move localization Dice to $0.1458$.
    \item \textbf{ForgeryNet (Full Framework)}: Integrating the complete multi-task architecture with asymmetric Focal Tversky loss ($\alpha=0.7, \beta=0.3$) achieves peak performance: \textbf{86.11\%} detection accuracy, \textbf{0.9347} ROC-AUC, \textbf{0.4125} Mean Dice, and \textbf{0.7718} taxonomy Macro F1.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tampering Taxonomy Performance and Confusion Dynamics}\label{subsec5_4}
Table~\ref{tab_type} details the per-category performance of Branch 3 across 108 forged test documents from unseen scribes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Character Addition} achieved near-perfect classification: $\text{Precision} = 0.9730$, $\text{Recall} = 1.0000$, and $\text{F1} = 0.9863$ (all 36 test specimens intercepted). This stems from distinct physical dynamics in Bengali orthography: squeezing an added glyph into existing handwriting disrupts character spacing, creating unnatural pen lifts, hesitation tremors, and acute stroke collisions across the continuous \textit{matra} that produce prominent high-frequency spikes in $\mathbf{R}$.
    \item \textbf{Splicing} attained balanced detection ($\text{Precision} = 0.6444$, $\text{Recall} = 0.8056$, $\text{F1} = 0.7160$), correctly capturing subtle inter-document ink-bleeding and scanner noise mismatches.
    \item \textbf{Copy-Move} achieved $\text{Precision} = 0.7308$ but moderate $\text{Recall} = 0.5278$, with 16 samples misclassified as Splicing. This confusion reflects an inherent constraint of localized patch-level processing: when the duplicated donor glyph resides in a different paragraph outside the $512 \times 512$ crop, the network observes only the transplanted recipient stroke. In the absence of the intra-page donor within the localized field of view, the local boundary edge features appear physically identical to spliced text.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we introduced \textbf{ForgeryNet}, an end-to-end dual-stream multi-task deep framework engineered for the simultaneous detection, pixel-level localization, and tampering type categorization of handwritten forgeries in Indic scripts, evaluated on Bengali documents. By addressing the severe downsampling degradation of prior models, ForgeryNet pairs native-resolution patch extraction with a complementary dual-stream backbone that fuses RGB visual representations with high-pass Laplacian noise residuals. A DenseNet-121 feature hierarchy augmented with bottleneck spatial self-attention and a 4-stage U-Net decoder with asymmetric Focal Tversky loss effectively overcomes severe foreground sparsity ($<1\%$ tampered ink). Evaluated under a strict, verified \textbf{writer-independent protocol} across 9 unseen scribes (144 test documents), ForgeryNet achieves an overall detection accuracy of \textbf{86.11\%}, an ROC-AUC of \textbf{0.9347}, a forged document sensitivity of \textbf{92.59\%}, a localization Mean Dice of \textbf{0.4125} (with Character Addition reaching \textbf{0.8388}), and a taxonomy Macro F1-score of \textbf{0.7718}.

\noindent
\textbf{Limitations and Future Work}: (i) \textit{Receptive Field Truncation}: By evaluating $512 \times 512$ patches extracted from high-resolution scans, distant intra-page donor-recipient copy-move correspondences outside the patch window cannot be resolved, explaining the observed classification confusion between Copy-Move and Splicing. Future work will investigate multi-scale graph neural networks to connect localized patch predictions across full-page layouts. (ii) \textit{Script Expansion}: While the dual-stream feature extractor and spatial self-attention modules are script-agnostic in formulation, empirical validation in this work centered on the Bengali script. Generalization across other complex Indic scripts (e.g., Devanagari, Gurmukhi, Tamil) represents an important direction for future research.
